Sizing a real edge without giving it back

The Kelly criterion tells you the bet size that compounds fastest. It does not tell you the bet size you can survive. On an account with a hard 3% drawdown limit, those two numbers are roughly two hundred times apart.

f* = p − q/b
f*fraction of current equity to risk
pprobability the trade wins
qprobability it loses, 1 − p
baverage win divided by average loss

What the formula is actually maximising

Most sizing rules try to maximise expected profit. That rule has no interior answer: if every trade has positive expectancy, expected profit is maximised by betting the whole account every time, and the whole account eventually meets a loser. Expected profit is an average over parallel worlds, and you only get to live in one of them.

Kelly maximises something different: the expected logarithm of equity, which is the same thing as the compound growth rate of the single path you actually walk. For a two-outcome trade where you risk fraction f of equity, win b times what you risked with probability p, and lose what you risked with probability q:

g(f) = p · ln(1 + f·b) + q · ln(1 − f) g is the expected log growth per trade. Differentiate, set to zero, and f* = p − q/b falls out.

The logarithm is what does the work. It punishes losses far harder than it rewards equivalent gains, because losing 50% requires making 100% to get back. Maximising log growth is not a risk preference someone chose; it is what compounding does to a sequence of returns.

The coin version

When a win pays exactly what a loss costs, b = 1, and the formula collapses to the version that circulates on its own:

f* = p − q = 2p − 1 A 60% coin at even money: 0.60 − 0.40 = 0.20. Risk 20% of the stack per flip.

That shorthand is only correct at even money. Trades almost never pay even money, and the payoff ratio moves the answer more than the win rate does.

It is a ceiling, and the far side of it is steep

The growth curve is not symmetric around its peak. Going under-sized costs you slowly. Going over-sized costs you slowly at first and then all at once, because past a certain multiple the compound growth rate turns negative while every individual trade is still profitable on average.

Fractional Kelly has a clean property worth memorising. If you size at k times the optimal fraction, you retain approximately k(2 − k) of the maximum growth rate:

Notice the symmetry in the middle rows. Half Kelly and one-and-a-half Kelly grow at the same rate, but one of them does it with a third of the variance. Everything above the peak is a strictly worse version of something below it. There is never a reason to be on the right-hand side of that curve.

The break-even multiple is exactly 2× in the continuous limit. With discrete bets it arrives sooner — between 1.7× and 2.1× for the profiles in this document — because large discrete losses compound worse than the smooth approximation suggests.

The mean tells the opposite story, which is the trap. Across those same simulated paths the 2× sizing has an enormous average final balance, propped up by a handful of runs that never had a bad streak, while more than half of all paths lost most of the account. An average that is dominated by outcomes you will almost certainly not get is not a forecast. It is arithmetic being misread.

Three assumptions that never hold

Kelly is a theorem, and it is correct. What fails is the input.

p and b are estimates, not constants

The formula treats the win rate the way physics treats a fair coin: known, fixed, and available to be plugged in. A trading win rate is a sample statistic with a confidence interval around it, and the interval is wide for a long time. Thirty trades leaves you unable to distinguish a 45% strategy from a 70% one. Since f* rises roughly linearly with p while the cost of overbetting rises quadratically, an estimate that is a few points too optimistic produces a position size that is badly too large.

Trades are not independent

Kelly assumes each trade is a fresh draw. Real positions correlate: three long positions in the same sector are one position wearing three tickets, and a regime change turns a scattered book into a single directional bet. Correlated trades collapse into a larger effective f than the one you sized.

The payoff ratio is an average, not a fixed multiple

Writing b as "average win over average loss" discards the distribution. A strategy with 1.6:1 average payoff built from many small wins and rare large ones behaves nothing like one with uniform 1.6:1 outcomes, and the second moment is precisely what Kelly is trading against. With a trade log in hand, the honest version is to maximise the average of ln(1 + f·r) over the actual return series rather than to compress it into two numbers first.

All three point the same direction. Every one of them means your true optimal fraction is lower than the formula reports, and none of them means it is higher.

The drawdown limit does the sizing for you

On a funded or prop account, the binding constraint is not growth. It is a threshold that ends the account when it is touched. Once a maximum drawdown is imposed, Kelly stops being a target and becomes a number you stay far beneath.

For continuously rebalanced Kelly betting there is a compact result: at k times the optimal fraction, the probability of ever drawing down to a fraction a of your peak is

P(drawdown to a) = a(2/k) − 1 At full Kelly, k = 1, so the exponent is 1 and P = a. A 50% drawdown has a 50% chance of happening. Eventually, it does.

Put a = 0.97 into that expression for a 3% limit and the arithmetic is brutal. Even at quarter Kelly the exponent is 7, and 0.977 is 0.81 — an 81% chance of breaching. To hold the breach probability near 20% you need roughly a fortieth of the optimal fraction.

Simulation over a finite horizon is kinder than the eventual-ruin formula, but not by much. What follows uses 200 trades, a 3% peak-to-trough limit, and a 20% tolerance for breaching it.

Putting it to work

  1. Compute the raw number honestly. Use p − q/b with your realised statistics, not your intended ones. Decide once whether break-even trades sit in the denominator, and never change it silently. Treat the answer as an upper bound you will not approach.
  2. Take the pessimistic end of your estimate. Build a confidence interval around your win rate and recompute f* at its lower bound. Under about a hundred trades that bound is so low it will often be near zero, which is itself the correct signal.
  3. Apply a fraction. Quarter Kelly on the pessimistic estimate is the standard starting point, and it is standard because the inputs are estimated rather than known.
  4. Then impose the drawdown limit, and let it win. Size so that the probability of touching your limit over your realistic trade count stays acceptable. On a 3% account this number is well under 1% per trade, and it will almost always be smaller than anything step three produced.
  5. Count correlated positions as one. Your effective risk is the risk of the whole book in a bad regime, not the sum of stop distances as if each trade were independent.
  6. Recompute on a schedule, not after a result. Re-estimate every fifty or hundred trades. Resizing after a good run is how a sound framework turns into the thing it was designed to prevent.

Two habits protect the whole framework. Size on current equity rather than a starting balance, which is what makes the bet self-correcting as the account moves. And never revise inputs upward during a winning streak, because a winning streak is exactly the sample most likely to be overstating the edge.

Traders who blow up rarely had a fake edge. They sized a real one as if it were a certainty, and the distribution was under no obligation to wait.
